# Editorial Notes

## Article role in the series

This article follows International Space Law and precedes Soft Law, Norm Entrepreneurship, and Informal International Governance. It should show how traditional international legal categories are being tested by distributed digital infrastructure, cross-border networks, private platforms, cyber operations, and data-driven power.

## Key framing

- Cyber operations do not exist outside international law; the hard questions concern threshold, attribution, evidence, remedy, and institutional capacity.
- Sovereignty, non-intervention, due diligence, use of force, state responsibility, countermeasures, human rights, cybercrime cooperation, and IHL all matter, but they do not always map cleanly onto networked infrastructure.
- Digital sovereignty should be treated carefully: it can protect regulatory autonomy and data governance, but it can also justify censorship, surveillance, and fragmentation.
- Private platforms, cloud providers, cybersecurity firms, and infrastructure operators are central governance actors even when international law remains state-centered.
- The article should avoid treating the Tallinn Manual as binding law while recognizing its influence as expert restatement and analytical framework.
