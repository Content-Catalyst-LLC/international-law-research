# Doctrine Summary: International Organizations and Legal Authority

International organizations are legal actors created by constituent instruments, typically treaties. Their legal authority depends on the terms of their founding instrument, delegated powers, institutional practice, implied powers necessary to fulfill their functions, privileges and immunities, and the legal effects attached to their decisions, standards, and recommendations.

The foundational authority is the ICJ's 1949 advisory opinion in *Reparation for Injuries*, which recognized that the United Nations possessed international legal personality and capacity necessary for the performance of its functions. That doctrine does not give every organization unlimited authority. It requires close analysis of the organization's mandate, functions, membership, decision-making rules, institutional practice, and applicable law.

Key questions for analysis:

1. What is the organization's constituent instrument?
2. Does the organization possess express powers, implied powers, or both?
3. Does the relevant act produce binding legal effects, soft-law influence, technical standardization, or political guidance?
4. What are the voting, consent, and institutional procedures?
5. Does the organization enjoy privileges and immunities?
6. Can responsibility be attributed to the organization, member states, or both?
7. What accountability mechanisms exist for affected states, individuals, communities, or peoples?

Legal authority in international organizations is therefore functional, delegated, institutional, and politically conditioned. It makes international law operational, but it also creates accountability and legitimacy problems.
